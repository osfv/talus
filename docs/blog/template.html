Why train our own

NULLSCAPE is a game whose worlds come from a machine-learning model. We wanted to own that model end to end: the training data, the architecture, the training loop, the evaluation and the inference code. No external generative API.

We started small on purpose. Every world in v1 is a 64 by 64 heightmap covering 4 km, with heights up to 1,200 m and a sea level. At that size one consumer GPU can train a model in an afternoon, so we could run experiments fast.

We compared VAEs, GANs, autoregressive transformers and diffusion models. We picked a conditional diffusion U-Net. Its training is stable, it covers the whole range of terrain rather than collapsing onto a few favourite maps, and you can steer it with properties like relief or water.

We named the model Talus, after the rock debris that collects at the foot of a cliff: terrain shaped by erosion, one grain at a time. This post covers Talus-1.

What we went through

The whole v1 cycle took two days, from an empty folder to a frozen, benchmarked model.

  1. Day 1, early

    Research and metrics first

    We wrote the evaluation before the model: 25 terrain metrics, a traversability check for a walking player, and a noise-floor protocol that compares learned maps against real maps the same way it compares real maps against each other.

  2. Day 1

    A procedural world generator

    Six terrain archetypes, erosion, global height normalization, and seeds that regenerate each map bit for bit. We rendered previews after every tweak until mountains looked like mountains.

  3. Day 1, afternoon

    The first training run crawled

    Training ran at under one step per second. On an 8 GB card, Windows silently spills video memory into system RAM when you run out, and everything slows by 10x or more. We capped PyTorch's memory and got 4.3 steps per second.

  4. Day 1, evening

    30,000 steps in 2.2 hours

    The model learned the big shapes within a few thousand steps. We added visual artifacts at every checkpoint, then continued to 40,000 steps to see if it kept improving. It did not.

  5. Day 1, late

    The sampler was hiding quality

    With 50 evenly spaced denoising steps the maps came out grainy. Placing more steps at low noise, called quadratic spacing, removed most of the grain without extra cost.

  6. Day 2

    Freeze, then benchmark everything

    We locked every checkpoint and the dataset read-only and scored five checkpoints and 20 sampler settings. We also stress-tested the controls and checked for memorization against all 45,000 training maps. That took about four GPU hours and 30,000 generated maps.

How good is v1?

We scored the model on 2,000 held-out maps it never saw during training or tuning. 100 means indistinguishable from real procedural terrain. Three non-learned baselines show the scale: blurred real maps, shaped noise, and a copy machine that returns the closest training map.

Where it falls short

Flat ground is noisy. Plains carry fine grain that real plains lack: about 3,000 times too much power at the finest scale and 4.6 times too many tiny pits. Ask the model for flat terrain and you get flat but rough ground.

Rugged ground is a little too smooth. Mountains, ridges and mesas lose detail at the 130 to 300 m scale, and mesa cliff edges come out soft. In the averaged spectrum the two errors cancel. We only saw them by splitting the numbers by terrain type.

One control does not work. The roughness dial, spectral beta, barely moves the output. Water only behaves when you also give the other properties.

We suspected mesas were the weakest terrain type. The data says plains. Mesas came second best overall.

Is it copying?

A model that returns its training data would ace most terrain metrics. So we searched all 45,000 training maps for the nearest match to every generated map, including rotated and mirrored versions, and pulled up the twelve closest cases. Each one shares a type with its neighbour, like a lone island or a gentle plain, and differs in structure. The difference panels on the right of each pair show it.

What you can use it for

v1 already produces maps you can walk around in. Every export carries the physical scale an engine needs.

Daily worlds from a seed

The sampler is deterministic per seed, so a date can become a seed and every player gets the same map that day. Leaderboards work without shipping map files.

Level layouts

The traversability module marks walkable ground, water and cliffs, and traces a long walkable route. Turn that route into the corridor of a run and steep slopes into walls.

Designer controls

Request a terrain type plus any subset of mean elevation, relief, slope and water. Leave the rest open and the model fills them in.

Straight into an engine

Export 16-bit PNG for Godot or Unreal, R16 for Unity, an OBJ mesh for quick previews, and a JSON sidecar with extent, cell size, max height and sea level.

Batch generation

Generate a whole day's worth of maps in seconds on a desktop GPU and serve them, rather than running the model on every player's machine.

A research baseline

The frozen benchmark and scorecard exist so every future version gets measured the same way, with a column for exactly how much it improved.

Want to walk one? The repository includes a playable prototype, demo/nullscape-demo.html. It's a first-person daily run through 30 v1 maps, with a low-res neon look, a timer and a beacon route to follow.

# generate 16 islands with lots of water, ready for Unity
nullscape sample --checkpoint runs/.../step_0030000.pt --archetype islands --prop water_fraction=0.6 \
  --steps 50 --spacing quadratic --guidance 2.0 --n 16 --formats png16,r16,obj --unity

What comes next

The next versions target the measured weaknesses, in order of cost. Each change must move its metric on this benchmark without hurting originality or control. The first one already landed: Talus-1.1 fixes the loss weighting and raises the scorecard average from 67.0 to 70.4, with slopes up 46%.

  1. Fix four export bugs. For example, Unity sizing doubles maps that already have a valid size (513 becomes 1025).
  2. Rebalance training (done: Talus-1.1). Removing the loss weighting that under-trained fine detail, plus learning-rate decay, cut the missing detail on rugged terrain by about 70%.
  3. Relative heights. Generate each map's shape at full scale, then rescale it with the requested elevation and relief, so flat maps get full precision.
  4. A better roughness control built on a measure that stays stable.
  5. Faster sampling through distillation to a handful of steps.
